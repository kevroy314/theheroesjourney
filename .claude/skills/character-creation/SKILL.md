# Skill: character-creation

One character exists that was designed rather than improvised: **Spite**. Read
`docs/CHARACTER-SPITE.md` for the finished artefact and this file for the
method. The whole cast — the traveller, the dog, the cat, Spite — is authored in
`tools/make_sprites.py` and regenerated by `python3 tools/make_sprites.py`.
Zero image quota has ever been spent on a character. That is not thrift, it is
a technical requirement, and §3 says why.

This is the opposite route from `docs/ADDING-ASSETS.md`, which turns a picture
into a prop. A prop is one silhouette seen once. A character is the same person
in twelve sprite cells and two portraits, and "the same person" is the part a
picture cannot give you.

## The order, and why it is this order

| # | step | the thing it decides |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Read the writing | what the character *is*; every pixel below is downstream of it |
| 2 | Place them against the cast | what makes them worth drawing at all |
| 3 | Generate or author | almost always author — decide it explicitly, not by default |
| 4 | Build the geometry table | one table, so the character cannot drift |
| 5 | Derive moods as a delta on it | two faces that are provably one man |
| 6 | Render the check sheets and **look at them** | the errors nothing else catches |
| 7 | Wire the contracts | portrait paths, sheet layout, placement |
| 8 | Write the design lock | `docs/CHARACTER-<NAME>.md` |

Steps 1 and 2 are hours of thinking and no code. Step 6 is where the time
actually goes. Do not reorder 1 and 3: deciding to generate before you know what
the character is, is how you end up hand-editing an off-palette render.

## 1. Read the writing first

**The writing comes first and the art's only job is to not contradict it.** For
Spite this was literal: `data/content/spite.json` (a weighted table of one-line
drive-bys with a mechanical effect each, and two named alters) and
`data/dialogue/spite.json` (an authored doorstep conversation) both existed and
shipped before there was a face. If your character has no writing yet, write it
first. A character designed from a silhouette outward has nothing to be checked
against and every drawing decision becomes taste.

The technique is to find the two or three lines that are load-bearing and spend
each one on a specific, defensible pixel decision. Spite's three, and what each
one bought:

| the line | what it rules out | what it buys |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *"Because I am what is left over when you talk yourself out of things."* | a hooded menace, a grinning demon, anything cute | he is the sneer, and the sneer is ashamed of itself |
| *"There is a great deal of me. I would like there to be less."* | a heroic bust filling its plate | thin, hunched, and composed **small and low in his own frame** — eleven of the portrait's fifty-six rows are empty door above his head |
| *"Four hundred and six. That's how many times you have opened that door."* | a pack, a bedroll, a belt kit | he has been sitting while you walked. He carries **nothing**. The traveller is equipped for a journey; Spite is not going anywhere |

That middle row is the one to copy. "Composition" is usually a house rule; here
it is a quotation. When someone later asks why the head sits low in the plate,
the answer is a line of dialogue, and the decision survives the next person's
taste.

The reverse also holds: the two alters in `data/content/spite.json` —
`kind` *"the one who remembers you"* and `wary` *"the one who remembers the
endings"* — are a **portrait spec written by the writer**, not a mood palette
chosen by the artist. Read what the data already names before inventing
expressions.

## 2. Design against the existing cast, not in isolation

**The only reason to draw a second character is to say something the first one
cannot.** Everything specific about Spite is stated *against the player*, and
each inversion is chosen to survive the zoom the game actually runs at
(`ZOOM 3` on 32px tiles, so a sprite pixel is three device pixels).

| axis | the traveller | Spite | why this axis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| crown | hood, 16 px wide, a suggestion of a face inside it | bare head, 12 px of hair, a whole face | the crown is the first thing the eye reads on a 32-px figure |
| arms | swinging **outside** his own outline every frame | hands in pockets, elbows out, arms **inside** the silhouette | open versus closed, said in the outline rather than the face, so it survives the zoom |
| the shared garment | scarf wrapped twice at the throat | the same scarf, same `WARM` ramp, same place on the body, hanging loose in two uneven ends | "I am part of you", drawn without drawing your face |

Pick the axes before you pick the pixels, and pick ones that read in the
**silhouette**. A difference that lives only in the face is a difference that
does not exist at 3×.

**The constraint that shaped him, and that still binds.** The Warden is already
*"your face, thirty years further on"* (`scripts/game/Outcomes.gd:198`). A
second character built out of the player's face would be the same device twice.
So: **Spite is not the player's face. He is the player's coat.** Before you
reach for resemblance-to-the-player as a design move, check it is not already
spoken for.

## 3. Author in code. Do not generate. Know exactly why.

The `game-art-pipeline` skill's hard finding is that **the model cannot hold an
identity across images** — sprite sheets come back as labelled contact sheets
with wandering column pitch and a silhouette that redraws every cell. The usual
conclusion drawn from that is "generate the portrait, author the sprite",
because a portrait is one image at one size.

**That conclusion does not survive contact with a character who has moods.**
Two portraits of one man in two moods *is* the identity-across-images problem,
with two cells instead of twelve. Generating `kind` and `wary` separately buys
two different men, and "the same person, twice" is the entire requirement.
Generating one and hand-editing the other is just authoring with an off-palette
starting point.

The second argument is the cast. Every figure is mixed from the ramps in
`data/themes/firstlight.json` (`COOL`, `WARM`, `LEATHER`, `SKIN`, and Spite's
`HAIR` / `SSKIN` derived from them). A quantised render does not land in those
ramps, and **a cast where one member is off-palette reads as a cast with a guest
in it.**

Reach for `game-art-pipeline` for backdrops, plates and lit atmospheric loops —
things seen once, where the model's strength (holding a scene while varying one
lighting property) applies. Do not reach for it for a person.

## 4. Build the geometry table

A figure is span tables plus small drawing functions over them, not free-hand
pixel calls. Spite's overworld sprite is `SP_HEAD_F`, `SP_NECK_F`, `SP_BODY_F`,
`SP_HEM_F` (front) and `SP_HEAD_P` / `SP_BODY_P` / `SP_HEM_P` (profile), each a
list of `(y, x0, x1)` rows; his portrait is one table, `P_HEAD`, twenty-two rows
of skull. Everything else is a function that reads those spans.

Three things every figure inherits, and none of them are optional:

- **The rim is derived from the alpha mask, not drawn** (`rim()`), so the
  silhouette is guaranteed closed whatever the pose does. Near-black, luma 9,
  8-connected — the same pass and the same colour every standing prop in
  `make_tiles.py` uses.
- **A baked contact shadow** (`contact_shadow()`), centred under the sole, in
  the tiles' one shadow colour at their one alpha (`SHADOW_A = 118`). Centred,
  not offset, because the character can stand anywhere. Without it he floats.
- **The sole sits on a fixed row in every frame** (`SOLE = 44`). Bob the whole
  figure and idle — the pose you see most — bounces. Compress the *upper* body
  by a pixel in the step frames instead, and draw the lifted foot short rather
  than somewhere new.

The value discipline is the part that is easy to skip and expensive to redo.
`COOL` has a **deliberate hole between luma 43 and 90** because sand (62), ice
(66), dune (68) and snow (72) all live there — cloth in that band dissolves into
the desert and the mountain. So cloth is pushed to either side of the ground
rather than spanning it. Filling Spite's lower half with `COAT_DK` cost 35% of
the figure "lost" on grass; the shipped split (`SP_BODY_F_K`) is 21%. There is
no single mid-tone that separates from a 38-point spread of ground, so **do not
look for one — bracket instead.**

## 5. Moods are a delta, and the delta is geometry

Portrait moods are **one geometry table plus a delta dictionary**, so the skull,
the hair, the nose and the scarf *cannot* drift between them. In
`make_sprites.py` the whole of it is:

```python
MOOD = {
    # head, shoulders, lid, brow row, brow tilt (left, right), curl
    "kind": dict(hdy=0, sdy=0,  lid=0, brow=20, tilt=(0,  0), curl=0, warm=1),
    "wary": dict(hdy=1, sdy=-2, lid=1, brow=21, tilt=(0, -1), curl=1, warm=1),
}
```

`portrait(mood)` composes `p_ground` → `p_coat` → `p_head` → `p_brows` →
`p_eyes` → `p_mouth`, and `p_head` is byte-identical between moods by
construction. Measured: **20.2% of plate pixels differ**, and it is one man.

**The light does not change between them.** Both halves are the same man in the
same doorway at the same hour. An early cut dimmed `wary` a step and **bought a
corpse rather than a mood** — the reader does not read "guarded", they read
"unwell". Note that `warm=1` is identical in both rows above: the delta table is
the enforcement, and a mood key that changes value is the smell.

What a face actually changes, in rough order of how far it reads:

| carries | how | reads at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| head down, shoulders up | `hdy +1`, `sdy -2` | before any feature resolves — a man with his head pulled in versus a man with his head out |
| collar up over the jaw | `collar_up` | silhouette |
| upper lid over the top row of the eye | `lid` | 112 px |
| **asymmetric** brows, one a row higher | `tilt=(0,-1)` | 112 px — this, not a curled lip, is what a sneer looks like from across a doorstep |
| two pixels of mouth corner, lifted | `curl` | 112 px, barely, and that is fine |

Asymmetry is the cheapest expression in the toolkit and the most reliable. A
symmetric change to both brows reads as surprise or as nothing; one brow reads
as an opinion.

## 6. The errors only looking catches

Four bugs in Spite got through every rule above and were found by opening the
check sheet. Each is now a comment at the line that fixes it. They are not four
unrelated mistakes — they are four instances of the same thing: **at this
resolution a shape you did not intend is as legible as the one you did, and the
unintended one usually wins.**

| what it looked like | what it was | the fix, and the general rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a **beak** on a grey skull, in profile | skin base at luma 160 against hair at 146 — the face was the brightest thing on the figure | base 128 (`SSKIN`, and `draw_brow()` records the traveller's version of the same failure). **Rank your value hierarchy across the whole figure, not per part.** A bright wedge sticking out of a pale dome is a bird's head at any zoom |
| **goggles** | brows drawn as a bar across a 12-px head; two dark bands across a small head | two pixels each with the forehead bare between (`sp_face_front`, `sp_hair_front`). **A full-width horizontal band on a small head is eyewear, not a feature.** |
| **teeth**, and a grin he does not have | a two-row mouth with a lit lower lip | one row, darkest value only in the middle (`p_mouth`). **Anything lit below a dark mouth line is teeth.** |
| a **moustache** | lifting the whole right half of the mouth for the sneer | two pixels of corner, up (`p_mouth`). **A raised half-mouth at 1:1 is facial hair.** |

The same failure mode, elsewhere in the same file, worth knowing before you hit
it yourself: a clean hair edge all the way round is a **helmet** (hence the
uneven fringe); a scarf widened a pixel a row is a **bib** (the accent is ~6% of
the figure by area and must stay about there, or the one saturated thing in a
cold palette becomes the subject); seven rows of bare neck is a **totem pole**
(`P_SHOULDER = 38`, four rows under the jaw); a full row of shade beside the
nose runs into the mouth and makes a **muzzle**; a lapel drawn as shading
vanishes — at this size a lapel is its **seams**.

When you review your own render, do not check whether each feature is correct.
Check what the *unintended* shapes are: squint, look at the silhouette alone,
and name what you see before you name what you drew.

## 7. The check sheets are the method

`make_sprites.py` writes underscore-prefixed contact sheets next to the shipped
art. **They are the deliverable of the drawing step; the PNGs are just output.**
Look at every one of them with the Read tool.

| sheet | what it is for |
| --- | --- |
| `assets/portraits/_spite_portraits.png` | both moods **at 1:1 on the real `panel_alt` colour, inside the real accent frame**, and again at 4× |
| `assets/sprites/_spite_x4.png` | the sheet with the cell grid ruled over it and columns and rows labelled — the layout contract, visible |
| `assets/sprites/_spite_ground.png` | the traveller **and** Spite side by side on all eighteen walkable materials, at `ZOOM 3` |
| `assets/sprites/_spite_walk.png` | the cycle in playback order, `1, 0, 2, 0` |

Two of those earn special mention.

**1:1 is the check that matters.** A face that only works at 5× is not a
portrait; it is a drawing of a portrait. The enlarged half of the sheet is for
finding *which pixel* is wrong after the small half has told you *that*
something is. If you only ever look at the 4× panel you will ship a face that
nobody can read on a phone.

**Side by side is the only test that matters for a second character.** He has to
be legible, *and* he has to not be the first one. `side_by_side()` puts both
figures in every cell for exactly that reason. Do it for the nearest existing
character, not just the player, once the cast has more than two.

### The numbers to hit

`python3 tools/make_sprites.py` measures these against the real tileset rather
than asserting them, and raises `SPITE CONTRACT FAILED` if the layout contract
breaks. Current figures, reproduced byte-identically from a clean checkout:

| measure | traveller | Spite | the bar |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| shoulders | 24 px | 22 px | differ from the cast; both fit inside 32 with a rim margin |
| rows of figure | 44 | 42 | |
| opaque pixels | 773 | 697 | |
| mean luma | 74.1 | 70.4 | close to the cast — this is not the axis you differentiate on |
| worst ground | hardpan, 16% lost | `grass_tall`, **20.5% lost** | keep "lost" under about a fifth on the worst ground; light ≥ ~45%, dark ≥ ~24% on every one of the eighteen |
| centre / sole | 15.5 / row 45 stable | 15.5 / row 45 stable | must be exact in all four facings, all three poses |
| alpha values | `[0, 118, 255]` | same | clear, shadow, figure. Nothing else |

The portrait's own numbers: 56 logical pixels doubled to the 112 that
`DialogueScreen._portrait()` reserves. Native 112 would make a portrait pixel
one device pixel where a world pixel is three, and read as a different game;
56×2 reads as "closer than the world". It also keeps the face inside a budget an
authored table can fill — 3,136 cells against the traveller's 773.

## 8. The contracts

Everything here is enforced by naming and by nothing else. **`validate_data.py`
checks that a critter's sprite sheet exists; it does not check that a mood has a
portrait.** Rename an alter and the art silently stops resolving — the screen
falls back to a lettered plate and no test fails. Be careful here.

**Portraits.** `Dialogue.portrait_path(speaker_id, mood)` returns
`res://assets/portraits/<stem>_<mood>.png`, where `<stem>` is `portrait` on the
speaker in `data/dialogue/<id>.json` and `<mood>` is the `mood` on the node.
`Dialogue.has_portrait()` gates it, and `DialogueScreen._portrait()` draws a
lettered 112×112 plate when the file is absent. **The moods are the alter ids in
`data/content/<id>.json`** — three things that must agree, in three files, with
nothing checking.

**The sprite sheet.** `assets/sprites/<sprite>.png`, and for a person 3 columns
× 4 rows of 32×48:

- Rows are `HJTileWorld.FACINGS` order: `down, up, left, right`.
- Columns are `neutral, step_left, step_right`. **Column 0 is neutral, not the
  first step** — so standing still needs no lookup table and no special case.
- The walk plays `WALK_ORDER = [1, 0, 2, 0]`. Playing `0, 1, 2` gives a limp;
  there is no neutral between the two steps.
- `right` is a horizontal mirror of `left`. A separately drawn right-facing set
  is only an extra chance for the two to drift.
- 32 wide because `TileWorld` centres with `(TILE - FRAME_W) * 0.5`: at 32 the
  offset is zero, the sprite grid *is* the tile grid, and the contact shadow can
  spread past the boots without being clipped.

**Placement.** A person walks in the world through the critter system.
`Critters.SHEET` defaults to the animals' `{cols: 4, rows: 4, w: 32, h: 32}`;
a species declares its own to be a person:

```jsonc
{ "id": "spite", "name": "Spite", "sprite": "spite",
  "sheet": { "cols": 3, "rows": 4, "w": 32, "h": 48 },
  "start": "waiting",
  "states": { "waiting": { "goal": "hold", "say": "..." } } }
```

`views()` carries `fw`/`fh` out to the renderer, so the renderer never learns
which species is which; a 32×48 figure is drawn from its feet like the player
and the extra height goes upward. A sheet with fewer than four columns has no
fidget frame and stands still rather than reading off the end of its own row.

**The files a whole character touches**, in the order you fill them:

| file | what goes in |
| --- | --- |
| `data/content/<id>.json` | the character: alters, weighted events, effects |
| `data/dialogue/<id>.json` | authored conversation; `speakers[].portrait` sets the stem |
| `tools/make_sprites.py` | the geometry tables, the mood delta, the check sheets |
| `tools/make_glyphs.py` | a 16 px `ICONS` entry if a placement or the UI names an icon |
| `data/content/critters.json` | species + `sheet` override, so it can stand somewhere |
| `data/content/interactables.json` | `verb`, `desc`, `reach`, `critter`, `effect` |
| `data/world/overworld.json` | `{x, y, type}` — where they actually are |
| `docs/CHARACTER-<NAME>.md` | the design lock (§9) |

Run `python3 tools/make_sprites.py`, `python3 tools/make_glyphs.py` and
`python3 tools/validate_data.py`, then `./test.sh`. `make_sprites.py` is
deterministic — a clean re-run is byte-identical, so a diff in
`assets/sprites/` or `assets/portraits/` after running it means you changed
something, including something you did not mean to.

The last four rows of that table are ordinary data plumbing, and the
`new-system` skill covers that shape in general — the schema/data/validator
ordering and the traps. Read it if the character needs behaviour rather than
just a body. If they live indoors, `interior-layout` owns the room they stand
in.

## 9. Write the design lock

`docs/CHARACTER-SPITE.md` is the template, and its shape is: the file table
first, then the reading, then the inversions, then the moods, then the
authored-not-generated argument, then the measured numbers, then the check
sheets, then what is still open. It exists because the reasoning behind a
character is more expensive than the pixels and is the part that gets lost.

Write it *after* the art, from the comments you left in `make_sprites.py`. Keep
the two in sync — the code comments are the primary record, because they sit at
the line they explain and cannot be forgotten in a rename.

## What is not solved

- **Portraits exist only for Spite.** There is none for the player and none for
  the Warden, and the Warden is the harder problem: he is the player's face, and
  the player's face has never been shown. `DialogueScreen` falls back to a
  lettered plate everywhere else.
- **The critter system is the only thing that walks a non-player figure.**
  `TileWorld` loads the player sheet directly; everyone else is a critter. That
  works — `sheet` makes the animal/person difference data — but a critter is a
  state machine over goals `hold`, `approach`, `retreat`, `wander`, so a
  character who needs to do anything else needs the general entity plane (#34),
  not more art.
- **Nothing validates the mood → portrait link.** See §8. A cheap win for
  whoever next touches `tools/validate_data.py`.
- **The cast is about to grow a lot** — #78 names the mountain woman, the
  unhoused man and the busybody; #77 names fifteen townspeople. Fifteen
  hand-authored geometry tables is not obviously the right answer. A shared
  human base table with a per-character delta — the same technique §5 uses for
  moods, applied one level up — probably is, and nobody has built it. If you are
  the second person to add a townsperson, build that instead of copying Spite's
  tables.
